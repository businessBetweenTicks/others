Care balance

Her circle fills it.
Nura spends it on her.

The approach, in one line
The balance fills as tokens, never as a figure — a meal, an afternoon, a visit — and the only person on the page who never sees it is her.
Why it is built this way
The brief asks for the balance to fill as small tokens — a meal, a visit, an afternoon — not as a dollar figure, and that instruction decides the whole section. Every default way of drawing a balance is a way of drawing money: the progress bar, the goal, the thermometer, the running total. Each one turns her recovery into a target somebody is behind on. So nothing here is a quantity. It is an inventory, counted in the things themselves, the way you count what is left in a cupboard.
The tokens are spaced so they read as separate objects rather than segments of a bar, and no two rows share a total — a shared denominator is exactly what makes a row look like progress toward a goal. Four dinners is not 66% of anything. Hollow is what has already arrived and solid is what is still coming, running left to right because that is the direction time runs. Depletion is not loss here: a token going hollow means somebody was fed.
The strip at the top is the brief's own sentence made into objects — a sister starts it, others add to it, and it turns into dinners that arrive. Two chips and a message. The first two are set in the system's sans because they are things people do; the third is the serif-page's message bubble, the same one the hero uses, because it is the only one of the three she ever sees. That change of voice is the transformation, and it does the work no arrow could.
The line under the strip is the section's rule: names travel, amounts never do. She is told who it came from, because knowing your sister fed you is the gift; she is never told what it cost, because that is what turns a gift into a price list.
The last of the three behaviours is the real argument. Somebody has to watch the balance and it must not be her. Telling her circle when it runs low — quietly, and only them — is what stops a gift from becoming a bill she is behind on. It is also the answer to the obvious objection about the panel above: on this page the reader is the circle, so showing it to them is correct. Showing it to her would not be.
Sequence · triggered once when the section is 40% in view
1
Heading, then the line beside it
Opacity 0 → 1, y +14px → 0, 620ms on ease-rise. The eyebrow goes first, alone, 220ms ahead. Same opening as every other section on the page.
2
The strip, read like a sentence
Three objects, never together. “Her sister starts it” fades up at 620ms on ease-rise. Its rule draws left to right over 620ms on ease-settle and “Eleven others add to it” lands as it arrives; the second rule follows the same way. Then nothing for 420ms before the message rises. That hold is the only place in the section where waiting does the argument's work — the beat between what was funded and what showed up is where the money stops existing. Close it and the strip reads as a checkout.
3
The balance filling, one token at a time
This is the beat the brief specifies. The heavy rule draws first, 620ms on ease-settle. Rows arrive top to bottom, 130ms apart. Inside a row the hollow tokens come first — what has already arrived — then the solid ones, 90ms apart, left to right, each its own 220ms fade. The row is never one element growing in width: a thing growing in width is a bar, and a bar is a budget. Row stagger is capped at 540ms so the longest row does not outrun the next one starting.
4
The three rules, and the third one alone
620ms each on ease-rise, 130ms apart, rules drawing under each. The third line — when it runs low, her circle is told — waits an extra 220ms after the second. It is the section's point and it is allowed the room. The closing serif line follows at 900ms, y +10px only.
Live behaviour · when someone actually adds
One new solid token appears at the end of its row, 420ms on ease-rise, and the row's names update. That is the entire event. No figure counts up, no total re-renders, no sound, no celebration. Somebody quietly put a dinner on the shelf; the page should behave the way a person would. When Nura spends one, the token goes hollow in place over 420ms — it does not leave the row, because what she was given did not stop having happened.
Never in this section
No currency symbol, anywhere, in any state — including empty states and error copy. No progress bar, percentage, goal, target, thermometer or countdown. No amount next to anyone's name, and no ordering of givers by how much they gave. No confetti or scale-up when a gift lands. No red, and no low-balance warning that can ever reach her screen. No “only two left” urgency written at her.
Reduced motion
prefers-reduced-motion: the arrow is drawn at rest, every mark is present, and the three rules cross-fade in at 220ms with no travel. Nothing is lost — the section is legible as a still, which is how it is designed and how it appears in the storyboard.
Accessibility
The marks are decorative individually and hidden from the reader; the row carries the meaning as a label — “Dinners that arrive: two already arrived, four still coming.” Plum on field clears contrast at this size, but the hollow token's hairline does not, which is why quantity is never carried by the token alone. The legend sits above the first row rather than below the last, so it is read before it is needed.
Copy flags for the client
The heading is the brief's, verbatim. The three things are the brief's three, in its order — dinners that arrive, a sitter who comes, a visit that gets booked — and the layout holds any three to five of them, breaking at seven.
One deliberate departure: the brief's line is “she never sees a wallet or a number.” Wallet is on the banned list in the packages sheet, and a banned word does not become safe by being negated — putting it on the page is still the only time a reader meets it. The idea survives intact as “she never sees a number,” and the three behaviour rules carry the rest. “Amount” and “cost” appear once each, in the negative, for the same reason.
