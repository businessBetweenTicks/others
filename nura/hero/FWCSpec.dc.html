First Week Check

The first week, handled.

The approach, in one line
Get out of the way of the copy — her account is the strongest asset this brand has, so the section is nothing but type, and the page's one piece of design is four things she did set beside eleven she never saw.
Why it is built this way
The brief's motion note is “quiet; let the words carry it,” and that is a licence worth taking literally. Every other section on this page argues with a diagram. This one has no diagram, no device, no panel, no accent — a heading, her account in the serif at 25px, one message bubble, and a link. It is the only section that is purely typographic, and the contrast is what gives it the “real space, not a footnote” the brief asks for. Nothing here needed inventing; it needed room.
The compressed version keeps three beats and cuts the rest: the message arriving and already knowing, the four taps and one honest answer, and the evening it produced a booked nurse. Everything trimmed is detail, not proof. The line that closes it — it was not an app I had to check, it was someone handling the part I did not have the room to handle — is the single best sentence in all three documents, so it is set largest, alone, above the link.
On the page itself the scene runs in full, verbatim, and then the one designed idea: her four actions in a narrow column beside Nura's eleven in a wide one. The columns are deliberately unequal and the short one is allowed to end early, leaving white space where her week would have been. That gap is the product. It is the same argument the hero makes with “she did none of it,” made once more with evidence.
What was cut, and why — read this before publishing
The source document carries material that should not reach a public page, and the design brief's own instruction is explicit: nothing about vendors, pricing math, clinical plans or medical records beyond the one data forward paragraph goes to whoever builds this. Three cuts were made on that basis, and none of them are stylistic.
1
The named state program
Her account names a specific county programme and its rollout schedule. On the page it reads “my state has a free nurse who comes to the house in my county” — true, hers, and it does not commit the client publicly to one vendor's coverage map or date. If legal wants the name back in, it is one string.
2
The clinical thresholds and the escalation protocol
Weight-loss percentages, day-14 triggers, tear grading, the fax to the pediatrician. A marketing page that prints thresholds is making a clinical promise in public, and this one is bought by a sister, not a clinician. The substance survives as one plain paragraph in a quiet panel: if she had said something that needed a doctor that day, it would have told her to call, given her the number, and sorted the ride and the cover.
3
“Why nobody else sells this”
Competitor positioning and hourly rates, ending with “that is the sentence to test in the debut.” That is a note to the founders, not page copy. Cut entirely. It is also the passage most likely to be pasted onto a page by mistake, which is why it is named here rather than quietly dropped.
One word kept on purpose
“App” is a word this brand otherwise avoids, and it appears twice — not an app, just a text, and it was not an app I had to check. Both are in her voice, both are negations, and both are doing the positioning the whole site depends on: naming the thing Nura is not. That is the opposite of the wallet on the care balance section, which was a banned word used in a sentence that survived without it. Here the sentence is the point. Kept, verbatim, and flagged so the decision is visible rather than accidental.
Motion · quiet, per the brief
·
The section on the landing page
Heading and eyebrow as everywhere else. Her paragraphs rise one at a time, 620ms on ease-rise, 220ms apart — slower and further apart than any other section, because the pace is the tone. The bubble arrives last, after a 420ms hold, on the same timing the hero uses for a message landing. The pull line and the rule beneath it come together. Nothing moves twice, nothing loops, and there is no parallax.
·
The page
The scene does not animate at all beyond a single fade on load — a long passage that reveals itself paragraph by paragraph as you scroll is an effect, and this copy does not need help. The one sequenced moment is the comparison: her four rows arrive first, 130ms apart, then a 420ms pause, then Nura's eleven at 90ms. Her column finishes early and visibly waits while the other keeps going. That pause is the entire piece of design on the page, and reversing the order or overlapping them loses it.
Never in this section or on this page
No photograph behind her words — a face turns testimony into an advertisement. No quotation marks, no pull-quote frame, no italic set of the whole passage; it is her account, not a review. No star rating, no “real customer”, no verification badge. No accent colour in the scene at all — the only colour on the page is the moss eyebrow, the plum label over Nura's eleven, and the button.
Accessibility and structure
The page is one article with a single h1 and two h2 sections, so it is readable end to end without the layout. The comparison is two lists, each with its own heading that carries the count in words — a reader who cannot see the columns still gets “she did four things” and “Nura did eleven”, which is the whole argument. Measure is held at 820px on the scene: long-form copy at 1440 without a measure is unreadable, and this is the one page people will actually read.
Copy flags for the client
The heading is the brief's. The scene is the First Week Check document's, verbatim except for the one programme name. The closing line is the document's own summary sentence.
Mine: the standfirst, the eleven-item breakdown (drawn from the behind-the-scenes passage and rewritten in plain words), the escalation paragraph, and the compressed three-paragraph version used in the landing section. All of it should be read back against the clinical partner before it ships, because this is the one page where a design decision and a medical claim are the same sentence.
